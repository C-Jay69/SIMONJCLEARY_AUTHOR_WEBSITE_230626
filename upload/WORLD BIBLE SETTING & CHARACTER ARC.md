**DUKE SAVAGE**

DUKE SAVAGE

\*\*Genre:\*\* Gonzo/Noir/Techno-Thriller    
\*\*Role in Story:\*\* Damaged but functional investigative narrator   
   
\*\*Key Traits:\*\* Cynical, hyper-observant, morally furious but emotionally exhausted; intelligent, sarcastic, allergic to bullshit; digital savant with a preternatural ability to follow money and digital patterns; perpetually disappointed by the world’s corruption, and angry at himself for still being surprised by it  

\*\*Conflict:\*\* Duke’s main conflict is his relentless pursuit of exposing systemic evil—especially digital and financial corruption—while wrestling with soul-crushing guilt over his estranged daughter Julie’s trauma, which he blames on his own crusading.

\#\#\# Character Profile

\*\*Background:\*\*    
Duke Savage was a prodigy with a keyboard and a curse with a conscience. He cut his teeth on the city’s digital arteries, tracing the flow of dirty money through a labyrinth of shell companies, offshore accounts, and encrypted whispers. His exposés toppled mayors, vaporized criminal syndicates, and made him a household name in the kind of households that kept panic buttons under the table. But the deeper he dug, the more the rot seeped into his own life.

His investigation into the “Heartland” child trafficking ring didn’t just rattle cages—it kicked the whole damn zoo. The blowback came home one night, shattering his daughter Julie’s sense of safety and sending her running from the wreckage of his crusade. She survived, but the light in her eyes didn’t. The immediate aftermath saw Duke infiltrating the Heartland ring, deep undercover, to get revenge. He went so deep he had to do things—unspeakable, soul-staining things—to keep his cover intact. The price: a gnawing addiction to fentanyl, a chemical ghost that still haunts his veins. He’s mostly managed to kick it, but the urge is always there, a shadow at the edge of every bad day. He’d call himself an unwilling but functional addict.

Duke’s been chasing ghosts and guilt ever since.

Now, he’s a digital wraith haunting the city’s underbelly, a legend to the desperate and a cautionary tale to the wise. He works alone, following the money, the data, and the lies, because stopping would mean facing the full, unfiltered weight of his failure.

\*\*Personality:\*\*    
Duke’s mind is a razor in a world made of fog. He sees through digital camouflage and human deceit with equal clarity. His wit is acid, his patience thin, and his tolerance for bullshit non-existent. He’s a man who’s seen the world’s guts and found them wanting, and he’s angry at himself for still expecting better. His moral outrage is volcanic, but it’s cooled by exhaustion and a self-loathing that gnaws at him in the quiet hours. Power, money, evil—they’re all just different flavors of the same poison, and Duke’s palate is jaded. The only thing that ever gets a flicker of respect is honesty, and even then, he treats it like a rare, endangered animal: with suspicion and a grudging nod.

\*\*Motivations:\*\*    
Duke doesn’t believe in justice anymore—he believes in exposure. He digs, he reveals, he wounds the beast, not because he thinks he can kill it, but because he can’t stand the thought of letting it feed unchallenged. Every case is a penance, every exposé a futile prayer for forgiveness he knows he doesn’t deserve. He can’t save the world, but he can make it bleed, and maybe, in the process, bleed a little less himself.

\*\*Kryptonite:\*\*    
Julie Savage. The one person who could ever make him hesitate, the one memory that can still cut him to the bone. Her absence is a wound that never closes, her silence a verdict he can’t appeal. Every investigation is haunted by her shadow, every victory soured by the knowledge that he can’t undo the past.

\*\*Voice:\*\*    
Duke’s narration is a cocktail of Hunter S. Thompson’s gonzo paranoia, James Ellroy’s staccato brutality, and Chandler’s weary lyricism—dry, sardonic, and laced with a bitterness that never quite curdles into self-pity. He never sounds impressed—by power, by money, by evil. Only occasionally by honesty, and even then, it’s more of a grunt than applause.

\*\*Sample Internal Monologue:\*\*    
\> The city’s digital veins pulsed with dirty money and dirtier secrets. I followed the patterns, the way a wolf follows a scent—nose to the ground, eyes on the horizon, never expecting anything but rot.    
\>    
\> The suits in the glass towers thought they were untouchable, but I’d seen their kind before. They all bled the same color when the truth cut deep enough.    
\>    
\> I used to think I was doing this for justice. Now I know better. I do it because I can’t stop, because the day I stop is the day I have to face what I let happen to Julie.    
\>    
\> Someone once asked me what impresses me. I told them: honesty. I haven’t seen much of it lately.

\*\*Key Rule:\*\*    
Duke never sounds impressed. Not by power. Not by money. Not by evil. Only occasionally by honesty.

**GHOSTS IN THE ASH — SYNOPSIS**  
Duke Savage is fifty-three, a former investigative journalist turned private investigator operating out of a cramped office above a pawnshop in Los Angeles. Estranged from his daughter Julie and struggling to stay ahead of the addictions and regrets that have accumulated across decades, Duke survives by taking the cases nobody else wants.  
When an encrypted message arrives warning that a young woman is running out of time, Duke begins searching for Sarah Chu, a twenty-seven-year-old data analyst who appears to have vanished without explanation. What he discovers is more disturbing than a disappearance. Sarah has been systematically erased from the administrative systems that govern modern life. Employment records, housing data, medical access, and financial history have all been manipulated to transform a living person into what the system classifies as administrative variance.  
The search leads Duke to Victoria Cross, a former insider attempting to expose a powerful network known as the Babylon Circuit. Operating through nonprofits, private equity firms, consultants, and data infrastructure providers, the Circuit has developed a method for quietly displacing vulnerable people from valuable property corridors while remaining entirely within the boundaries of the law.  
As Duke follows the trail, he discovers hundreds of victims, each subjected to carefully engineered disruptions designed to make them disappear without ever becoming the victim of a traditional crime.  
Duke meets Marisol Reyes, a home care aide whose insulin access has been deactivated because his queries made her a liability, and Raoul Cruz, an operative whose cousin's displacement first mapped the Circuit's mechanism. Marisol becomes the named plaintiff in the federal complaint Duke's civil rights attorney Park is building.  
Reviewing extracted Beacon Forward data at 4 a.m., Duke finds his daughter's name in the Circuit's proximity marker log. SAVAGE, J. — EMPLOYEE. Current. Julie has been inside the surveillance architecture for two years without knowing it. Duke fumbles the cylinder of his .38. A round rolls to the baseboard. He leaves it there.  
Victoria discloses the full truth: she engineered Duke's involvement because he was the one variable the Circuit couldn't quietly resolve. Together they construct the chain of custody Park needs to file.  
When Duke reaches Julie — already a data forensics expert who has mapped her own Circuit flag — she doesn't thank him. She tells him he chases collapse because that's where he's strongest. He was protecting her from the wrong world.  
The federal complaint is filed. A preliminary injunction freezes the infrastructure corridor. Duke wins — partially. The mechanism is wounded but doesn't die. It updates.

**PROLOGUE: OMAHA, 1999**

The house was a rental on Fontenelle Boulevard, the  
kind of property that existed in the kind of neglected rental where weeds choked the lawn and the furnace groaned like it was already spring.  
Two bedrooms, a yard that had given up, and the same  furnace that ran three months behind the weather.   
The man who lived there had been renting it for eleven months under a lease that was due for renewal in six weeks, though the lease would not be renewed.   
That had already been decided by people who would never see the property or know its address. The decision existed in a different document, in a different city, filed under a category that had no official name.  
The street was quiet at 2 a.m. It was the kind of quiet  
that a Midwestern winter produced — not peaceful,  
but sealed. The cold kept people inside and kept sound  
close to the ground and kept the city's overnight  
processes running without witnesses.There were three of them. They came in a car without  
plates, which was the kind of operational detail that  
spoke to experience rather than planning — men who  
had done this before and knew which details mattered  
and which ones didn't. They parked two blocks east.  
They walked with their hands in their pockets and  
their breath making small clouds that the dark  
swallowed immediately.  
The man inside the house had a daughter. Eight years  
old. She slept in the back bedroom with a lamp on —  
not from fear, but from habit, the specific comfort of a  
child who had learned that darkness was negotiable  
and light was a decision you could make for yourself.  
The lamp threw a warm stripe under the closed door.  
In the hallway, it looked like a signal.  
The man was at his desk when they came through the  
door. He had been at his desk for six hours. He had a  
transcript in front of him, forty-three pages,  
double-spaced, the testimony of a man who had  
agreed to speak on the condition that his name would  
never appear in print and his family would never be  
endangered and the story would run before anyone  
with a reason to stop it could do so. The man at the  
desk had made all three promises. He had meant  
them. He had not yet understood that promises  
required infrastructure to keep, and that his  
infrastructure was already compromised, though hewould understand this within the week — first  
through the phone call from his editor, then through  
the item in the Omaha World-Herald, fourteen column  
inches, single-vehicle accident on Highway 36, driver  
pronounced dead at the scene, no suspicious  
circumstances.  
The door came in on the second impact. The frame  
held longer than they'd expected — the man who'd  
installed it had done his job well, which was the kind  
of anonymous competence that never appeared in any  
record but occasionally altered the course of events by  
thirty seconds.  
Thirty seconds was enough.  
The man at the desk heard the first impact and was  
standing by the second. He was not a large man but he  
was not a frightened one — he had spent three years  
developing the specific calm of someone who had been  
in rooms where the truth was dangerous and had  
learned to locate the exits before he needed them. He  
put the transcript in the furnace on his way through  
the kitchen. That was the right decision and he knew it  
and made it without hesitation, which was the last  
clean decision of the evening.  
What followed was not clean.It lasted four minutes. It moved through the kitchen,  
the hallway, the front room. It was the kind of violence  
that was not intended to kill — the instructions had  
been specific on this point, because killing journalists  
in 1999 still produced the kind of institutional  
attention that was expensive to manage, and the  
organisation that had issued the instructions was,  
above all things, careful about expense. The intention  
was communication. The message was: you are not  
protected. Your promises are not infrastructure. The  
story does not exist.  
The message was delivered.  
What had not been factored — what existed in no  
document, no operational brief, no risk assessment —  
was the child.  
She appeared at the top of the stairs at the two-minute  
mark, drawn by the noise the way kids are drawn to anything that sounds wrong, a siren song of parental panic that ripped her out of sleep before she was even awake.  
She stood at the top of the stairs in her nightgown with the lamp light behind her and she looked down at the  
hallway.  
She saw men. She saw movement. She saw a struggle  
in the dark in which the shapes were indistinguishable from each other — the men who had come through  
the door and the man who lived there, all of them  
reduced by the poor light and the motion and the  
terror of the moment to the same category of thing.  
Threat. Violence. The world coming through the door.  
She could not tell them apart.  
This was noted. Not in a document — there was no  
document for this. It was noted in the way that  
experienced men noted details that were not in their  
brief but that told them something useful about the  
variable they were managing. The child at the top of  
the stairs, unable to differentiate. The lamp behind her.  
The stripe of light under her door.  
The variable had a daughter.  
The daughter had a wound now that had no name yet,  
that would take years to surface and longer to  
understand, that would express itself as distance and  
then as the specific vocabulary of a woman who had  
learned to say what she meant because the alternative  
— the elaborate interior architecture of not saying it  
— had been built by her father and she had watched  
what it cost him.  
The men left. The car without plates was two blocks  
east and then gone.The man on the floor of his hallway got up. This was  
also noted — that he got up, that he always got up,  
that this quality in him was both his value and his  
problem. A man who didn't get up was a solved  
problem. A man who got up was a variable that  
required ongoing management.  
He would require ongoing management for the next  
twenty-five years.  
The transcript was ash.  
The informant would be dead in six days.  
The editor who killed the story would receive a wire  
transfer of $40,000 to an account in the Cayman  
Islands, a transaction that would be documented in a  
ledger that would not surface for twenty-five years, and  
when it did surface it would surface as a line item in  
an invoice reviewed by a man in a room in Los Angeles  
who would sit very still for a long moment before he  
understood what he was reading.  
The story did not run.  
The organisation that had issued the instructions  
noted the outcome in its records under the category it  
used for resolved operational risks: MANAGED.  
VARIABLE CONTAINED. MONITORING ONGOING.The child went back to her room.

She left the lamp on.
